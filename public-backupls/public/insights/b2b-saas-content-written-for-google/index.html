Most B2B SaaS content is written for Google. That's the problem.

The failure isn't bad writers. It's that nobody in the commissioning chain is accountable for whether a buyer found the page useful.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 6 min read

There is a particular kind of B2B SaaS article that is impossible to argue with and impossible to remember. It defines the term in the title. It lists benefits. It has a section headed "Best practices" containing four practices that are, at most, fine. It ends by suggesting you consider your unique business needs.

Nobody set out to write that. It is the output of a process, and the process is working exactly as designed. The problem is what it was designed to optimise.

Follow the chain that produced the article

Trace a typical B2B SaaS blog post backwards from publication and you find something like this.

A keyword tool produced a list sorted by volume and difficulty. Somebody filtered it for relevance, which in practice means removing the terms that were obviously off-topic. A brief was written specifying a word count, a primary keyword, three secondary keywords, and a set of headings derived from the pages currently ranking. A writer — often a capable one — was given that brief and a deadline. An editor checked it for grammar, tone and keyword placement. It shipped.

At no point in that chain did anyone ask what a buyer would need to understand in order to move one step closer to a purchase. The question is not withheld out of cynicism. It simply is not anybody's job.

Look at what each participant is actually measured on. The keyword researcher is measured on identifying opportunity. The brief writer is measured on producing briefs. The writer is measured on delivering to spec, on time. The editor is measured on quality of execution against that spec. The channel owner is measured on traffic and rankings.

Every one of those people can do excellent work, and the aggregate output can still be commercially inert, because the specification they are all serving describes a page that ranks rather than a page that helps someone decide.

Distribution is easy to measure, usefulness is not

This is the mechanism underneath the whole thing.

Rankings, sessions and impressions are available tomorrow morning, attributable to a specific URL, and visible on a dashboard your CEO can read without training. Whether a piece changed a buyer's understanding is knowable only in fragments, months later, through sales conversations nobody logs systematically.

Given a measurable proxy and an unmeasurable objective, organisations optimise the proxy. That is not a marketing failing; it is what organisations do. But it means the entire commissioning process quietly drifts toward the thing it can see.

The drift compounds. Once you are optimising for distribution, the ranking pages become the specification. And the ranking pages were, in most B2B categories, produced by exactly the same process at a competitor. So the brief describes an average of the existing average, and the resulting article is a slightly better-executed version of what already exists — which is precisely the article that has no reason to be read by someone who has already read three of them.

What it looks like on the page

You can identify these pieces without knowing anything about how they were commissioned. A few reliable tells:

  • It defines a term the reader already knows. Anyone searching "SaaS content strategy" has an operational grasp of both words. Three hundred words defining the phrase exists to satisfy a structural expectation, not a reader.
  • The headings mirror the competition's headings. If four pages ranking for a query share the same six sections in the same order, none of them was structured by someone thinking about the problem.
  • There is no claim in it. Nothing a reasonable practitioner would push back on. Which means nothing that could be wrong, which means nothing that could be useful.
  • The examples are hypothetical. "Imagine a SaaS company that..." Real examples require access to real situations, and the commissioning process did not budget for acquiring any.
  • It ends by recommending you consider your specific context. The reader came for help with their specific context.

None of these are writing problems. A better writer given the same brief produces a more elegant version of the same inert page.

The two-reader test

Here is a way to catch this at the brief stage, which is where it is cheap to fix.

Before commissioning any piece, name two specific readers.

Reader one is the person who will find this through search. What did they type, and what state of knowledge does that query imply? Not "someone interested in content strategy" — someone who typed a specific string at a specific moment for a specific reason.

Reader two is someone in an active buying process at your company who will be sent this link by a colleague, or will find it after a sales call. What do they need to believe that they do not currently believe?

Then ask the only question that matters: what does this piece have to contain to be worth their time?

Most briefs cannot survive this. The topic turns out to serve reader one and not reader two, or the reverse, or neither — at which point you have learned something useful for the cost of ten minutes rather than the cost of an article.

When a topic serves both, the brief writes itself, and it looks nothing like a list of headings copied from the current results.

Where the search-first approach is right

I want to be fair to the other side, because the argument above can be taken too far.

Search demand is real information. When a thousand people a month type a question, that question is genuinely being asked, and answering it well is genuinely valuable. Ignoring search data in favour of writing whatever the founder finds interesting is its own failure mode, and it produces libraries that nobody ever finds.

There are also categories where the search-first approach works cleanly: high-intent comparison and alternatives queries, integration and compatibility questions, pricing and implementation questions. In those cases the searcher's intent and the buyer's need are the same thing, and optimising for distribution optimises for usefulness at the same time.

The failure is not using search data. It is letting search data be the only input, and letting distribution metrics be the only accountability. A topic map built from search demand, buyer evaluation questions, and topics where your depth is defensible will beat one built from any of the three alone.

What this means for a startup or SME

If you have a small team, this matters more to you than it does to a company with forty marketers, for an unfair reason: you cannot absorb the waste.

A large content operation can publish sixty inert pieces a year and still hit its numbers because a few pieces work and the rest are noise inside a big budget. At fifteen people, twenty inert pieces is your entire content investment for the year, and you will conclude that content does not work in your category.

Three practical changes, in order of how much they are worth:

  1. Put the two-reader test in the brief template. A brief that cannot name both readers does not get commissioned. This costs nothing and kills most of the problem.
  2. Make one person accountable for whether pieces are useful, not just published. Usually that means someone who sits in on sales calls. Useful is unmeasurable in the abstract but obvious in a specific conversation.
  3. Report on two numbers, not one. Distribution and consequence. Even a crude second number — how many of last quarter's pieces were sent by a salesperson to a live opportunity — changes commissioning behaviour immediately.

The goal is not to write for buyers instead of for search. It is to stop treating those as the same brief written by the same process, and to make one person responsible for the half of the equation that nobody currently owns.

Where this leaves you

If your content is technically correct and commercially quiet, the brief is usually the thing that's broken, not the writing.
