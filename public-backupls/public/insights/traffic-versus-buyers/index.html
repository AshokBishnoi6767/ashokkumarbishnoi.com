The difference between content that gets traffic and content that gets buyers.

Two programmes, identical dashboards, opposite commercial outcomes. The measurement layer is where the lie lives.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 5 min read

  3. Rebuilding the measurement layer
  4. In defence of traffic
  5. What this means for a startup or SME

All insights

Two companies run content programmes for a year. Same category, similar budgets. At the end of it, their analytics dashboards look almost identical: organic sessions up meaningfully, keyword rankings improved, time on page respectable, a healthy library of published work.

One of them has built an asset that is now producing pipeline. The other has spent a year buying an audience of people who will never purchase anything, and does not know it yet.

Nothing in the standard reporting stack distinguishes these two outcomes. That is the problem this piece is about — not that marketers chase vanity metrics out of vanity, but that the measurement layer they inherited genuinely cannot tell these two programmes apart.

Why the dashboards agree

Web analytics counts events: a session, a scroll, a page, a duration. Every one of those is a fact about behaviour and none is a fact about intent.

Consider two visitors to the same article. The first is a student writing a dissertation. The second is a platform engineer at a company that has just been told to solve this exact problem by Q3.

The student reads carefully for eleven minutes, scrolls to the end, and visits two more pages. The engineer skims for ninety seconds, finds the specific paragraph that answers their question, and leaves to go and ask their manager about budget.

Every engagement metric you have rates the student higher. The engineer is the entire point of the programme. Optimise for the metrics and you will systematically produce more content for students.

This is not a hypothetical failure. It is the ordinary mechanism by which content programmes drift toward high-volume, low-intent topics: those topics genuinely score better on the numbers being reported.

What actually differs between the two programmes

If the dashboards agree, what is different?

Who arrives. Not how many. A programme built on informational queries at the top of a category attracts researchers, students, competitors and people two years from a purchase. A programme built on evaluation-stage questions attracts fewer people, most of whom are in a buying process.

What the piece does to their understanding. A useful piece moves a reader from one state to another: they now believe something they did not, or they can now do something they could not. Traffic-optimised content usually confirms what the reader already knew, which is comfortable and changes nothing.

Whether sales can use it. This is the sharpest single discriminator I know. In a buyer-oriented programme, salespeople send links from the content library unprompted, because a piece answers an objection they hit weekly. In a traffic-oriented programme, sales does not know the library exists.

What happens next. Whether there is a next step at all, and whether it makes sense for the reader who just finished. A well-built piece knows who is reading it and what they should reasonably do at the end.

Rebuilding the measurement layer

You do not need attribution software to fix this. You need three numbers most teams are not collecting, all obtainable without new tooling.

1. Sales usage

How many times last quarter did a salesperson send a link to a piece of your content to a live opportunity? Which pieces?

Collect this by asking. A monthly message in the sales channel — "which of our content did you send to anyone this month?" — produces a usable answer in five minutes. It is unglamorous and it is the most predictive content metric I have encountered, because a piece a salesperson chooses to send is a piece that does work in a real buying process.

2. Presence in closed-won deals

For deals that closed, did the buyer touch content before the first conversation? Which pieces?

If you have a CRM with any session history, this is a report. If you do not, it is a question in the deal-review meeting: did they mention reading anything? Even a crude, self-reported version of this number reorders your content priorities immediately, because a small number of pieces show up repeatedly and they are rarely the ones with the most traffic.

3. Query intent mix

Take your top fifty queries from Search Console and classify each one as informational, evaluation-stage or commercial. Then look at the proportion of your traffic coming from each.

A programme that is 90% informational is not necessarily wrong — but it should be a deliberate decision with a stated timeline for when the mix changes, not an accident nobody has noticed.

These three numbers take a couple of hours a quarter to assemble and will tell you more than any dashboard about which of the two programmes you are running.

In defence of traffic

The argument above can be pushed too far, and the over-corrected version is also a failure.

Traffic is not meaningless. It is a necessary precondition — content that nobody reads cannot influence anyone, and a programme that only ever targets bottom-of-funnel evaluation queries will run out of those queries quickly, because there are not many of them and your competitors are already there.

There are also legitimate reasons to pursue volume that have nothing to do with immediate pipeline: building topical authority so that later commercial pages rank, establishing a category position before demand exists, or building an audience you can reach directly.

The distinction that matters is whether the volume is instrumental or terminal. Traffic pursued as a step toward something, with a stated theory of how it converts to commercial outcome and a date by which you expect to see that, is a strategy. Traffic pursued because it is the number on the report is not.

What this means for a startup or SME

This matters more at your size for the same reason most content problems do: you cannot absorb a year of the wrong thing.

A large company running the traffic-optimised programme still gets some pipeline from it, because the absolute numbers are big enough that even a poor conversion rate produces deals. A fifteen-person company running the same programme gets a traffic graph and a budget conversation.

Three things.

Start collecting sales usage this month. One question in one channel. Within a quarter you will know which pieces do work and which merely exist, and that knowledge changes what you commission far more than any analytics configuration.

Report two numbers to leadership, not one. Distribution and consequence. Even if the consequence number is crude — pieces sent by sales, deals that touched content — having it in the report changes behaviour, because people optimise for what is reported.

Before commissioning anything, write down what would have to happen for it to have worked. Not the metric target. The event: a salesperson sends it, a specific query brings a specific kind of reader, someone in evaluation stops worrying about a specific objection. If you cannot name the event, you will fall back on traffic by default, because traffic is what gets measured when nothing better was defined.

Traffic is a means. Everyone knows this and almost nobody's reporting reflects it, which is why so many content programmes end their second year with an impressive graph and an uncomfortable question.

Where this leaves you

If nobody can name which pieces produced pipeline last year, the measurement layer is the first thing to fix.
