B2B SaaS

Small SaaS companies don't need more content. They need better content.

The standard playbook was written by teams of forty. Run it with a team of one and it doesn't underperform — it fails differently.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 6 min read

The B2B content playbook you have read was written by companies that could afford it. Pillar pages and topic clusters, a documented editorial process, quarterly content audits, a distribution motion across four channels, a measurement framework with attribution.

All of it is good advice. All of it assumes a content team of five to ten people, a design resource, and an analytics function.

Applied at fifteen employees with one marketer who also owns events, email and the website, it does not underperform gracefully. It fails in a specific and predictable way: the team attempts the full programme, cannot sustain any part of it properly, and concludes after eight months that content does not work for them.

The playbook was not wrong. It was written for a different constraint set, and constraints are not an inconvenience to be worked around. They are the design input.

What actually breaks at small scale

Four things, in the order they usually go.

Cadence goes first. A publishing schedule that depends on one person's uninterrupted time collides with a product launch, and the gap becomes a habit. Six weeks later, restarting feels like admitting failure, so it does not happen.

Quality goes second, and quietly. Under time pressure, the research step is the one that gets compressed, because it is invisible in the output until someone knowledgeable reads it. The pieces still ship. They are just no longer built on anything.

Distribution never starts. The plan had four channels. In practice, publishing consumes the entire budget and pieces go out with a single LinkedIn post. The content is fine and nobody sees it.

Measurement collapses into pageviews. Proper attribution requires setup time nobody had, so the reporting defaults to the number that is available by default. Then decisions get made on that number, which sends the whole programme toward traffic rather than buyers.

Notice that none of these is a talent problem, and none is solved by trying harder. They are the predictable outputs of running a plan designed for ten people with one.

Plan from the constraint, not the ambition

The alternative is to start from what you can actually sustain and design backwards.

Ask one question honestly: how many hours a month does content genuinely have, on a bad month? Not a good month. The month with the launch, the conference and the two people on holiday. That number is your real capacity, and everything should be planned against it.

For a lot of small B2B technology companies, the honest answer is somewhere between fifteen and thirty hours. That is not enough for twelve pieces. It is comfortably enough for two properly built ones, or one substantial one plus distribution.

Two properly built pieces a month, sustained for two years, is forty-eight pieces of real substance. That beats a two-hundred-piece library assembled from whatever fit the time available, on almost every measure that matters, including the one where your own sales team can find something worth sending.

What to cut, specifically

Cutting requires knowing what earns its place. My ranking, for a team under twenty people:

Keep: the pages that explain your product. Homepage, product pages, the one technical page a serious evaluator reads. These are permanently in front of every buyer. If the constrained budget only covers one thing, it covers this. A confusing product page costs you deals every week; a missing blog post costs you nothing.

Keep: one proof asset. A single well-built case study, from a real customer interview, aimed at the objection that kills your deals most often. This is the highest-leverage single document a small B2B company can own, and most do not have one.

Keep: your founder's point of view, on a low cadence. One substantial piece a quarter. Not replicable by competitors, not commoditised, and it costs an hour of founder time plus writing.

Cut: publishing cadence as a target. Publish when there is something worth publishing. The consistency argument is real for large operations building topical authority at scale. At your size it mostly generates filler.

Cut: multi-channel distribution. Pick one channel where your buyers actually are, usually LinkedIn or a specific community, and do it properly. Four channels done badly reach fewer people than one done well and cost four times as much attention.

Cut: top-of-funnel awareness content, for now. This is the least popular item on the list. Broad awareness content has the longest payback period and the weakest attribution, which makes it exactly the wrong thing for a team that needs to demonstrate content is working before the budget gets reallocated. It becomes correct later, once something is compounding.

The one-asset quarter

A structure I have seen work for genuinely small teams.

Each quarter, pick one thing worth doing properly. Not one piece — one asset, meaning something with enough substance to be reused across formats for months.

Quarter one might be a rebuilt product page plus the messaging framework underneath it. Quarter two, a case study from a real customer interview. Quarter three, a substantial argument from your founder. Quarter four, a technical guide that becomes the reference page for a query your buyers actually search.

Around each asset, plan roughly six weeks of derivative work: the LinkedIn posts, the sales email, the shorter explainer, the section on an existing page that now links to it. Derivative work is cheap because the thinking is already done, and it is where most small teams leave value on the table.

Four assets a year with proper derivative distribution outperforms fifty standalone posts. It is also achievable on fifteen hours a month, which is the point.

Where this advice is wrong

Two cases.

If you are in a category with high, well-defined search demand and low technical difficulty — a crowded horizontal market where buyers search obvious terms — then volume genuinely does compete, and a small team may be better served by a productised content operation than by depth. You will be competing on efficiency rather than substance, which is a legitimate if uncomfortable strategy.

And if you have raised money specifically to build a content moat and have the runway to staff for it, then build the full programme. The advice here is about matching design to constraint. If your constraint changes, the design should too.

What this means for a startup or SME

Three things to do this month.

Calculate your bad-month capacity honestly and write it at the top of your content plan. Every subsequent decision gets checked against that number.

Audit what you have before adding to it. With fifteen or twenty posts this takes an afternoon, and it is the cheapest it will ever be. You are looking for the small number of pieces that are doing anything, so you can do more of that and stop doing the rest.

Pick this quarter's one asset and protect the time for it. The protection is the hard part. Whatever it is, it needs to survive a launch, a conference and someone's holiday, which means it needs a deadline that is not the end of the quarter.

Small teams do not lose because they have less budget. They lose because they run a plan built for a budget they do not have, and then draw the wrong conclusion when it does not work.

Where this leaves you

Constraints aren't the reason your content underperforms. Ignoring them is.
