B2B SaaS

Your SaaS website doesn't have a traffic problem. It has a clarity problem.

Both problems produce almost identical dashboards. Here is how to tell them apart before you spend another quarter on acquisition.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 5 min read

  2. A diagnostic you can run this week

  5. What this means for a startup or SME

A traffic problem and a clarity problem look nearly identical from inside the analytics. Sessions are flat or fine. Bounce rate is unremarkable. Demo requests are lower than anyone wants. The obvious conclusion is that you need more people at the top of the funnel, so the budget goes to acquisition.

Sometimes that is right. Often it is the single most expensive mistake available to a B2B SaaS company, because buying more traffic for a page that under-explains the product means paying to send more qualified strangers away with a slightly wrong idea of what you do.

The two problems have different fixes and roughly the same symptoms. Here is how to tell them apart before you commit a quarter's budget.

Why the two get confused

Standard web analytics measures whether people arrived and whether they left. It does not measure whether they understood.

That gap is where the misdiagnosis lives. A visitor who reads your homepage carefully, concludes you do something adjacent to what you actually do, and leaves is recorded identically to a visitor who was never a fit. Both are one session and one bounce. The first is a clarity failure and represents lost revenue. The second is a targeting outcome and represents nothing.

Because the two are indistinguishable in the data, teams default to the interpretation that has an obvious remedy. Traffic problems can be solved by spending money. Clarity problems require a decision about what the company actually claims, which is harder, slower, and involves disagreement among people who outrank the person raising it.

A diagnostic you can run this week

Five tests. None requires new tooling and all of them can be done in a few days.

1. The five-second test

Show your homepage to eight people who do not work in your industry and who have never seen it. Five seconds, then take it away. Ask two questions: what does this company sell, and who is it for?

You are not looking for perfect answers. You are looking for whether the answers cluster. If eight people give you six different descriptions of your product, buyers are doing the same thing at scale, and no amount of traffic fixes it.

2. The sales-call opening

Listen to five recorded first calls. Time how long your rep spends explaining what the product is before the conversation moves to the prospect's situation.

If it is consistently more than about three minutes, the website did not do its job. The prospect arrived with a gap the site should have closed. That is a clarity problem stated in your rep's own time, and it is expensive in a way that never appears in a marketing report.

3. The internal consistency test

Ask four people — a founder, a rep, an engineer, a marketer — to write one sentence describing what the company sells, separately, without conferring.

Compare the four sentences. Real disagreement here is diagnostic gold. It means the website is not failing to express a clear position; it is faithfully expressing an unresolved one. No copywriter can fix that, and hiring one before you resolve it wastes both the budget and the writer.

4. The search-query audit

Look at what people actually type on your own site search, and at the queries in Search Console that land on your product pages.

People searching your site for a capability that is on the page they are already reading is a clarity signal. So is a product page attracting queries describing a different category. Both mean the page is not communicating what it thinks it is communicating.

5. The competitor swap

Take your homepage headline and value proposition and put your closest competitor's name at the top.

If the page still reads as accurate, you have not written a positioning statement. You have written a category description, and the category is doing the work that your differentiation should be doing.

Reading the results

Some rough scoring.

Three or more tests fail: you have a clarity problem, and any acquisition spend before you fix it is subsidising your competitors' win rate.

Only tests 3 and 5 fail: the problem is upstream of copy. This is a positioning decision the leadership team has not made, and the website is merely where the indecision has become visible. Fix the decision first; the pages take a fortnight after that.

Everything passes and demo requests are still low: now you may genuinely have a traffic problem, or a product-market fit problem, or an offer problem. Acquisition spend is a reasonable next move, and you have ruled out the cheaper explanation first.

That order matters. Clarity is the cheapest thing to fix on this list and the one most likely to be the cause. Testing it first costs a week.

What clarity actually means here

Clarity is not simplicity, and it is not brevity. Both are frequently mistaken for it.

Simplifying a complex product usually means removing the specificity that made it credible to the buyer who was going to purchase it. Technical evaluators do not want a simpler page; they want a page that respects what they already know and gets to the part they do not.

Clarity is ordering. It is deciding what a stranger has to understand first, second and third, and refusing to say the second thing before the first has landed. Most confusing B2B pages contain all the right information. They present it in an order derived from how the product was built rather than how a buyer forms a judgement.

A useful sequence for a product that is genuinely hard to explain:

  1. The situation the reader will recognise as theirs. Not the problem in the abstract — the specific operational circumstance.
  2. What the product does about it, in one sentence, in plain language, with no metaphor.
  3. Why that is different from the two alternatives they are already considering, including doing nothing.
  4. Evidence that it works.
  5. Everything else — features, integrations, architecture — for the reader who is now interested enough to want it.

Most B2B homepages start at four or five and never do one or three at all.

What this means for a startup or SME

You have one advantage over larger competitors here and it is worth using: you can change your homepage this month. A company with a brand team, a design system and four approval layers cannot.

Two practical notes.

First, run the diagnostic before you commission anything. If test 3 fails badly, do not hire a writer yet. Get the four people in a room and resolve the sentence. A writer can facilitate that conversation, but they cannot substitute for it, and a writer who claims otherwise is selling you a document rather than an outcome.

Second, be careful about who validates the result. The people closest to the product are the worst judges of whether a page explains it, because they cannot un-know what they know. The five-second test exists specifically to route around that, which is why the eight people have to be strangers.

Traffic is expensive and clarity is cheap. Establishing which one you actually need takes about a week, and it is the highest-return week most B2B marketing teams could spend.

Where this leaves you

If the diagnostic points at clarity, the fix is a messaging decision before it is a writing job.
