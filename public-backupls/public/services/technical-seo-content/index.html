The problem

There are two ways a B2B technology blog fails, and they look nothing alike from the inside.

The first is invisibility. You publish thoughtful pieces about topics nobody searches for, aimed at an audience that would love them if it knew they existed. Traffic stays flat. Everyone concludes SEO doesn't work in your category.

The second is worse, because it looks like success. You hire a competent generalist agency, they build a keyword list, and within a year you have ninety articles and a traffic graph pointing up. Then someone asks how many customers came from it, and the room goes quiet. The traffic is real. It is students, job seekers, competitors, and people three years from a purchase. Your buyers were never in it.

Both failures share a cause: nobody involved understood the product well enough to know which topics a buyer actually researches before spending money. So the topic list defaulted to whatever a keyword tool ranked highest, which is exactly the list your competitors got from the same tool.

What that costs is not just the retainer. It is the two years of compounding you didn't get, while a competitor who chose better topics now owns the search results your sales team has to work around.

Who this is for

  • B2B SaaS companies whose category has real search demand and real technical depth.
  • AI, data and machine-learning companies where the interesting topics require reading a paper, not a press release.
  • Cybersecurity companies selling to practitioners who can smell a writer bluffing within two paragraphs.
  • DevTools companies whose buyers are developers and whose content therefore has to be correct, not merely fluent.
  • Technical service businesses competing against companies with far larger content budgets and no product knowledge.

What I do

I build a topic map from three inputs, not one: real search demand, your buyers' actual evaluation questions, and topics where depth creates an advantage a generalist can't copy.

Then I write the pieces. Not brief them and hand off — write them. A brief written by someone who won't have to write the article is an optimistic document.

I do not promise rankings. Nobody honest does. What I promise is that each piece has a defensible reason to exist: genuine demand, genuine depth, and something in it that the pages currently ranking don't have.

Process

  1. Understand the product and the buyer

    Documentation, demo, sales-call themes, and the questions that come up during technical evaluation. This is the step that separates useful topic maps from generic ones.

  2. Map demand against relevance

    Search demand is one axis. Commercial relevance is the other. High-volume, low-relevance topics get deliberately declined — and I'll show you the ones I'm declining, so it's a decision rather than an omission.

  3. Sequence the roadmap

    Which pieces come first, which support them, and how they link. Publishing order matters more than most teams assume; the wrong order wastes a quarter.

  4. Research and write

    Primary sources, product reality, and where relevant, input from your own engineers. Technical accuracy is a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have.

  5. Review and adjust

    What ranked, what converted, what didn't, and what the next quarter should change as a result.

Questions

Can you guarantee rankings?

No, and neither can anyone else. What I can do is make sure every piece has a defensible reason to rank: real search demand, genuine depth, and something in it that competing pages don't have.

Do you do the keyword research or do we?

I can do it, or work from yours. Either way the output is a topic map tied to commercial relevance rather than a spreadsheet sorted by volume.

How much of this is writing versus strategy?

Both, and they're hard to separate usefully. A brief written by someone who won't write the article tends to produce a worse article.

What if our product is very technical?

That's the point of the service. The harder the product is to explain, the fewer writers can cover it credibly, and the less competition there is for the topics that matter.

Send me your current topic list

I'll tell you which pieces I think are worth writing, which ones are volume with no buyer behind it, and what's missing entirely. That conversation is free and usually short.
