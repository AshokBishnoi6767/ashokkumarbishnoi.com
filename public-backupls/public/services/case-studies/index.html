The problem

B2B deals rarely die because the buyer decided your product was worse. They die because the buyer could not build a confident enough case internally, and the safest available decision was to do nothing this quarter.

Your champion has to argue for you in a meeting you are not in. What they take into that meeting is whatever you gave them. If that is a three-paragraph case study consisting of the words "streamlined", "seamless" and "trusted partner", they are going in unarmed.

Meanwhile the specific thing that would have won — that you have done this before, at their scale, in their industry, with the integration they're worried about, and here is what actually happened including the part that was difficult — sits unwritten because nobody scheduled the customer call.

Weak proof content is expensive in a way that never shows up on a line item. It shows up as longer sales cycles, more discounting, and deals that quietly convert to "no decision" after four months of your team's time.

Who this is for

  • Companies with happy customers and nothing written about them. The most common version, and the easiest to fix.
  • Companies selling into evaluation committees, where a technical reviewer and a finance approver need different documents.
  • Companies whose sales team has stopped sending the existing case studies, which is the clearest signal you have that they don't work.
  • Companies entering a new segment and needing credible proof that they belong there.

What I do

I interview your customer. Not a form, not a questionnaire relayed by an account manager — a recorded conversation where I can follow the interesting thread when it appears.

That is where the useful material lives: what they tried first, why it failed, what they were worried about before signing, what surprised them afterwards, and what they would say to someone in their position now. None of that survives a survey.

Then I write it as a story with a decision at its centre, structured so a reader can extract the argument in ninety seconds or the detail in ten minutes, depending on which kind of reader they are.

Deliverables

  • Sales narratives

Process

  1. Pick the right customer

    Not the biggest logo. The one whose situation most resembles the deals you're currently losing.

  2. Prepare with your team

    What sales needs this document to do, which objection it should defuse, and what the internal account context is before I speak to anyone.

  3. Interview the customer

    Thirty to forty-five minutes, recorded, structured around the decision rather than the product.

  4. Write and verify

    Draft, internal review, then customer approval. Every number and quote gets confirmed by the person it belongs to.

  5. Cut the versions

    Full case study, one-pager, and the two or three paragraphs sales can paste into an email. Same source, different jobs.

Questions

Do you run the customer interviews?

Yes. A case study built from a form your customer filled in reads like a form your customer filled in.

What if the customer won't share numbers?

Then the story carries the piece. A specific, well-told account of what changed operationally is more persuasive than a rounded percentage nobody believes.

What if the customer won't go on record at all?

Anonymised case studies work when the detail is real: the industry, the scale, the situation, the change. What breaks them is vagueness, not anonymity.

How long does a case study take?

Typically two to three weeks end to end, most of which is scheduling the customer rather than writing.

Name the deal you lost last quarter

Tell me why it stalled and who else was in the room. That usually identifies the exact document you're missing.
