The problem

Most small technology companies do not have a content strategy. They have a publishing habit and a spreadsheet, and they have never been asked to defend the difference.

You can tell from the symptoms. Content starts enthusiastically in January and stops in April. Nobody internally owns it, so it lands on whoever has the least urgent week. Sales doesn't use any of it, because nobody asked sales what would be useful. And when someone finally asks which pieces are working, the honest answer is that nobody knows, because success was never defined for any individual piece before it was commissioned.

The expensive part isn't the wasted budget, although that's real. It's the compounding you didn't get. Content is one of the few marketing investments that appreciates — a good article written three years ago is still working. But it only compounds if the pieces connect to each other and point somewhere. Ninety disconnected posts are not an asset. They are ninety separate small expenses that happen to live on the same domain.

And there is a specific trap for companies with fifteen or twenty published posts: it feels too small to bother auditing. That is precisely when auditing is cheap. Fixing a library of two hundred posts costs ten times more and usually ends in a deletion project.

Who this is for

  • Technology companies of roughly 10–200 people with a small marketing team and no dedicated content lead.
  • Teams about to increase investment, who want the map before they spend the budget.
  • Teams whose publishing has stalled and who suspect the problem is structural rather than a motivation issue. It usually is.
  • Founders considering a first content hire, who need to know what that person should actually be responsible for.

What I do

I audit what exists, work out who you are actually trying to reach and what they need to believe before they buy, and build a topic architecture where each piece has a defined job and a defined relationship to the others.

Then I define how you'll measure it — before publishing, not after, because a measurement framework invented retrospectively is just a story about why the numbers are fine.

This is the decision layer. It is deliberately written so that execution can be done by your team, by me, or by someone else entirely. A strategy that only works if I write everything is a bad strategy for a company with an internal team.

Deliverables

  • Content audit — what exists, what works, what should be improved, merged, redirected or retired
  • Audience and problem mapping
  • Topic architecture — pillars, supporting content and how they link
  • Buyer-journey mapping, from first search to signed contract
  • Editorial roadmap in publishing order, with reasoning
  • Measurement framework tied to pipeline rather than pageviews

Process

  1. Audit

    Every published piece assessed against performance and relevance. Most audits find that a small number of pages carry almost everything, and that a large number should never have been written.

  2. Understand the buyer

    Who decides, who blocks, what they search, what they need to believe, and what your sales team hears repeatedly.

  3. Build the architecture

    Pillars, supporting pieces, internal links, and the commercial destination each cluster points to.

  4. Sequence the roadmap

    What to publish first and why. Ordered so early pieces make later ones stronger rather than competing with them.

  5. Define success

    Per piece and per cluster. If a piece can't have its job written in one sentence, it comes off the roadmap.

Proof

Questions

Is this a document or an engagement?

The audit and roadmap are documents. They are only worth commissioning if someone is going to execute against them, which is why most strategy engagements here roll into execution.

How long does a content audit take?

Usually two to three weeks, depending on how much content exists and how much analytics access is available.

What if we only have fifteen blog posts?

Then the audit is short and the roadmap matters more. Small libraries are easier to fix, and fixing them early is much cheaper than fixing two hundred posts later.

Do you work with our existing writers?

Often. A strategy that only works if I write everything is a bad strategy for a company with an internal team.

Send me your blog and your analytics

I'll tell you what I'd audit first and roughly what I expect to find. If your library is small enough that a full engagement would be overkill, I'll say that too.
