The problem

Almost everything filed under "thought leadership" in B2B technology contains no thought and leads nothing. It summarises a trend everyone has already read about, adds three cautious observations, and ends with a paragraph about how the future will be exciting.

This happens for a structural reason, not a talent one. The people with genuine opinions — founders, principal engineers, heads of product — are the least available people in the company. So the work gets delegated to whoever has capacity, and capacity is inversely correlated with expertise. The result is content that is safe, competent, and indistinguishable from what your competitor published last week.

Then it gets reviewed. Marketing softens the argument so it can't be quoted against you. Legal removes the specific example. Someone suggests adding a section for balance. What arrives on the other side is a piece with no claim in it, which is the one thing thought leadership cannot survive.

The cost is that you competed on features against companies buyers already trusted. Trust is what shortens sales cycles, and a founder who has published a real argument for three years starts every conversation ahead of one who hasn't.

Who this is for

  • Founders who have earned strong opinions and have never written them down.
  • CEOs whose company sells to a market that buys on trust before it buys on features.
  • Technical leaders — CTOs, principal engineers, heads of security — whose credibility with a technical audience is a commercial asset.
  • Marketing leaders who need an authority programme that doesn't depend on the founder finding writing time.

Poor fit: companies that want thought leadership as a new label for the same product content, or that need every published sentence pre-approved by four departments. The approval process is usually the thing that has to change first.

What I do

Extraction, then argument, then writing — in that order.

Most founders don't think they have a point of view. What they have is a set of strong reactions: things that annoy them about how their industry works, decisions they made against consensus, things they believed five years ago and no longer do. Those are points of view. They just haven't been separated from the operational context they live in.

My job is to pull them out in an interview, test whether they hold up, find the argument underneath, and write it so it sounds like the founder rather than like marketing. Original expertise is the entire product here. If a piece could have been written without ever speaking to your founder, it isn't thought leadership.

Deliverables

  • Point-of-view development — what this person actually believes, and what they're prepared to say publicly
  • Topic strategy
  • Expert interviews and input sessions
  • Long-form articles
  • LinkedIn adaptations, planned from the start rather than retrofitted
  • An authority content roadmap

Process

  1. Position, not topics

    A first session on what this person believes that their market mostly doesn't. That disagreement is the raw material for a year of content.

  2. Interview

    Forty-five to sixty minutes per piece. Recorded. My job in that hour is to ask the questions that make them say the thing they'd never think to write down.

  3. Find the argument

    What's the claim, what's the evidence, where does it break, and what does someone reasonable say against it? A piece that can't survive its own counterargument isn't ready.

  4. Draft in their voice

    Including the way they build an argument and the things they refuse to say. Voice is structural, not a matter of vocabulary.

  5. Adapt and distribute

    The long piece plus the LinkedIn versions that stand alone rather than teasing a link.

Proof

Thought leadership, whitepapers and long-form content across 600+ content projects for 80+ clients in SaaS, AI, data, cybersecurity and IT. Some ghostwritten pieces are published under other author names, which is the nature of this work.

Questions

How much of the founder's time does this take?

Roughly forty-five to sixty minutes per piece, in an interview. If a programme needs more than that from a founder, it isn't designed properly.

Is this ghostwriting?

Usually. The ideas are the founder's, the argument is developed together, and the drafting is mine. Some of my published work appears under other names for exactly this reason.

What if the founder doesn't have strong opinions yet?

They almost always do. What they lack is a way to separate the opinion from the day-to-day operational context they think in. That extraction is most of the work.

Can you adapt long-form pieces for LinkedIn?

Yes, and it should be planned from the start. A long article that was never designed to be broken apart makes a poor LinkedIn post.

Tell me what your founder is tired of arguing about

That sentence is usually the first article. Send it over and I'll tell you whether there's a year of content behind it or a single good post.
