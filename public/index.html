Most technology companies don't have a content problem. They have a translation problem.

Somewhere in your company, a small number of people understand exactly what your product does, who it is for, and why the alternatives fall short. That understanding almost never survives the trip to your website.

It gets flattened into feature lists. It gets handed to a writer who has never opened your documentation. It gets diluted into blog posts written for a search engine rather than a buyer. And then the company concludes it needs more content, which is roughly the same as concluding that a leaking bucket needs more water.

What that costs is not abstract. It is the evaluator who read your product page, decided your product does something adjacent to what it actually does, and never filled in the form. It is the deal your competitor won because their case study answered the question your prospect was really asking. It is eighteen months of publishing that produced traffic and no pipeline, and a marketing budget you now have to defend in a board meeting.

What I do differently

I read the documentation. I sit through the demo. I ask the questions your engineers find obvious and your buyers find decisive.

Then I write something a technical evaluator will respect and a non-technical buyer can follow — which is a much harder brief than either one alone.

Why this works

  • Technical depth

    A computer science background and hands-on experience across SaaS, AI, data, cybersecurity, IT and emerging technology. I don't need your engineers to simplify the product before I can write about it — which is the step where most technical content quietly loses its accuracy.

  • Commercial thinking

    Every piece has a job: traffic, qualified leads, sales enablement, trust, positioning, or clearer product communication. If a piece can't name its job, it shouldn't be commissioned, and I'll say so before you pay for it.

  • Research first

    Products, audiences, competitors and primary source material, before a word gets written. The difference between content that sounds informed and content that is informed shows up in exactly the places your buyers are paying attention.

  • Range

    Blogs, website copy, case studies, whitepapers, thought leadership, email, briefs and the rest of the B2B catalogue — 600+ projects across 80+ clients. Range matters because the right answer to your problem isn't always the format you came in asking for.
