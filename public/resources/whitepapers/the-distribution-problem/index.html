The Distribution Problem

Why distribution deserves equal investment to content creation, and what a real distribution plan looks like beyond hitting publish.

Ashok Kumar Bishnoi·September 11, 2026·5 min read
Executive summary

Genuinely excellent content with no real distribution behind it is functionally invisible. Distribution isn't a final step after content is finished — it's half the work, and most teams treat it as an afterthought. Why distribution deserves equal investment to content creation, and what a real distribution plan looks like beyond hitting publish.

01 — The pattern

You Can Publish the Best Content in Your Industry and Still Be Invisible.

A recurring, quietly demoralizing pattern in content teams: a genuinely strong piece — well-researched, well-argued, better than most of what's in the category — gets published, and almost no one sees it. The team's instinct is to conclude the content wasn't good enough. Often the actual problem is that "publish" was treated as the finish line, when distribution was always the other half of the job.

Input

A specific list of people and…

A specific list of people and channels this content is genuinely relevant to, decided before publishing, not improvised afterward.

02 — The framework

What actually closes it

a connected network representing real distribution — a specific relevant list, direct outreach, a plan for proven content.

Output

Direct outreach, not just a social…

Direct outreach, not just a social post. Sending a genuinely relevant piece directly to the specific people it would help — a customer, a partner, a journalist covering the space — does more than a single scheduled post ever will.

Output

A plan for content that has…

A plan for content that has already proven itself. Strong-performing content deserves a second and third distribution push, in different formats or channels, rather than being left behind the moment something newer gets published.

03 — Diagnostic

Where to look first

  1. For the last piece of content your team was genuinely proud of, how much deliberate distribution effort went into it after it was published — and did that effort match how good the content actually was?
