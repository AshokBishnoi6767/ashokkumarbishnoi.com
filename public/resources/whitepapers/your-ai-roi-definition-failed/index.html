AI ROI

A framework for defining AI return on investment before a pilot begins, so success or failure is measured against something that actually matches what the pilot was meant to prove.

Ashok Kumar Bishnoi·September 11, 2026·5 min read
Executive summary

Before declaring an AI pilot a failure, it's worth checking whether the pilot actually failed — or whether it was measured against a return-on-investment definition that never fit what the pilot was designed to do. A framework for defining AI return on investment before a pilot begins, so success or failure is measured against something that actually matches what the pilot was meant to prove.

01 — The pattern

Your AI Pilot Didn't Fail. Your ROI Definition Did.

A striking number of AI pilots get labeled failures for a reason that has nothing to do with the technology: nobody defined what success meant before the pilot started, so success got defined afterward, informally, usually against whatever metric made the result look disappointing. That's not a technology failure. That's a measurement failure wearing a technology failure's clothes.

Input

Deciding, before the pilot starts, what…

Deciding, before the pilot starts, what kind of pilot it is. A pilot testing whether a known efficiency gain is achievable deserves a traditional ROI model. A pilot exploring a genuinely new capability deserves a learning-based standard — did we learn what we needed to learn, cheaply and quickly enough to make a real decision?

Output

Naming the specific metric in advance

Naming the specific metric in advance, not the category. "Improve efficiency" isn't a metric. "Reduce the time from request to first draft by X" is — and it's the kind of specificity that prevents an after-the-fact metric from being chosen to fit whatever result showed up.

Output

Setting a threshold in advance, including…

Setting a threshold in advance, including for stopping. A pilot with a pre-agreed "we'll stop if we don't see X by this date" is far more honest than one that runs indefinitely because nobody defined what "not working" would even look like.

03 — Diagnostic

Where to look first

  1. For your current AI pilot, what specific number, defined before it started, would tell you clearly whether it worked — and if that number doesn't exist yet, what's actually going to happen when someone asks?
  2. Deciding, before the pilot starts, what kind of pilot it is. A pilot testing whether a known efficiency gain is achievable deserves a traditional ROI model. A pilot exploring a genuinely new capability deserves a learning-based standard — did we learn what we needed to learn, cheaply and quickly enough to make a real decision?
