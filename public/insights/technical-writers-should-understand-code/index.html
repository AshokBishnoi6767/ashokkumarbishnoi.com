Technical writers should understand code.

Not to write it. To notice when the explanation you have been handed doesn't hold together.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 6 min read

The title overstates it slightly, so let me narrow it immediately. Technical writers do not need to ship code. They do not need to pass a coding interview. Most of them will never open a pull request.

What they need is enough technical literacy to notice when the explanation they have been handed does not hold together. That is a much lower bar than "can code" and a much higher one than the industry currently applies.

Because the alternative to noticing is transcribing. And a writer who can only transcribe will faithfully reproduce every error, ambiguity and half-truth that arrives in the source material, in polished prose that makes the errors harder to spot.

What technical literacy actually means here

Concretely, for someone writing about B2B software:

  • Being able to read API documentation and understand what the thing does, not just what it is called
  • Knowing the difference between authentication and authorisation, and noticing when a page uses them interchangeably
  • Understanding what a webhook is, why polling is different, and what that difference means for a customer
  • Being able to follow an architecture diagram well enough to ask where the data goes
  • Knowing roughly what happens when a system is under load, and why "scalable" is not an answer
  • Recognising when a claimed integration is a real integration and when it is a CSV export with a schedule

None of this is advanced. All of it is learnable in months rather than years. And each item on that list corresponds to a specific class of error I have watched go to production on real B2B websites.

The errors this catches

Four categories, roughly in order of how much damage they do.

The category error

A page describes the product as belonging to a category it does not belong to, usually because the category is better known and someone thought it would aid comprehension.

The cost is asymmetric and severe. Non-technical readers are unaffected. Technical evaluators — the people who decide whether you get shortlisted — read it, conclude you are a weaker entrant in a category you are not actually in, and compare you against products you do not compete with. You lose on a comparison that was never relevant.

The impossible claim

A capability described in a way that could not work as stated. Real-time synchronisation with a system that only exposes a batch API. Zero-configuration setup for something that manifestly requires credentials and field mapping. No-code for a workflow that needs conditional logic.

Usually the underlying product is fine and the claim is a compression of something more nuanced. But a technical reader does not know that. They know the page says something that cannot be true, and their next question is what else on this site is not true.

The silently omitted constraint

The most commercially expensive, because it does not surface until late. The page says the product integrates with a system, and it does — for one direction, for one object type, on the higher plan.

A writer who understands the domain asks "in both directions?" during the briefing. A writer who does not asks nothing, publishes the sentence, and the constraint emerges in week three of an evaluation, at which point you have not just lost the deal but damaged your credibility with an evaluator who will remember.

The buried differentiator

The inverse error and the most common of the four. The genuinely interesting thing about the product — the architectural choice that makes it faster, the approach that avoids a problem competitors have — is invisible on the website, because the writer could not tell it was interesting.

The engineers know. They just do not know it is a selling point, because to them it is simply how the thing works. Surfacing it requires someone who can recognise a non-obvious technical decision when they hear one.

The objection: naive readers are useful

There is a real argument against all of this, and it goes something like: a writer who does not understand the product is a proxy for the buyer who also does not understand it. Their confusion is diagnostic. A writer who understands too much will write for engineers and lose everyone else.

The premise is right and the conclusion is wrong.

The premise is right because expert blindness is real, and the closer you are to a product the worse you get at explaining it. That is why the five-second test uses strangers.

The conclusion is wrong because it confuses two different things: not understanding the product, and choosing not to foreground technical detail. The first is a limitation. The second is an editorial decision, and you can only make it if you understood the material in the first place.

A writer who understands the architecture and then decides the homepage should not mention it has made a judgement. A writer who omits it because they never grasped it has made an omission that happens to look similar. The difference shows up on the product page, in the technical FAQ, and in every conversation where a detail actually matters.

The useful position is understanding the product thoroughly and then writing for someone who does not — which is harder than either pure naivety or pure expertise, and is the actual job.

How to check for this when hiring

Portfolios are poor evidence here, because good editing hides comprehension gaps well.

Better: give a candidate a page of your own documentation and ask what questions they would need answered before writing about it. This is far more revealing than a writing sample. You are looking for questions that show they followed the mechanism — what happens if this call fails, is this synchronous, what is the actual constraint here — rather than questions about tone, audience and word count.

A second test: hand them a competitor's product page and ask what they think is genuinely differentiated versus what is table stakes. Someone with domain literacy will separate the two quickly. Someone without will restate the page's own claims back to you.

Neither test takes more than twenty minutes and both are far more predictive than another sample of clean prose.

What this means for a startup or SME

If your product is genuinely hard to explain, that is a moat and you should treat it as one.

The pool of writers who can cover your category credibly is small. That is inconvenient when you are hiring and enormously advantageous once you have hired, because your competitors face the same shortage and most of them will settle. The content they publish will be fluent, slightly wrong in places, and unable to surface anything that a generalist could not have inferred from the marketing site.

Three practical consequences.

Pay more per piece for fewer pieces. Technical literacy commands a premium, correctly. The premium is smaller than the cost of publishing something a technical evaluator dismisses.

Give your writer real access. Documentation, a sandbox account, thirty minutes with an engineer. A literate writer with no access produces the same output as an illiterate one, and you have wasted the premium you paid.

Have an engineer review for accuracy, not for style. Two different reviews with two different briefs. Engineers asked to review prose generally rewrite it into documentation. Engineers asked "is anything here wrong?" answer precisely and quickly.

The point of technical literacy is not to make content more technical. It is to make it correct, and to make sure the thing worth saying about your product actually gets said.

Where this leaves you

The harder your product is to explain, the fewer writers can cover it credibly. That is a moat if you use it.
