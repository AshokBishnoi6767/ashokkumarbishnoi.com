B2B SaaS

Stop publishing 12 SaaS blogs a month.

Do the arithmetic on your own cadence. Most teams have never done it, and the number is worse than they expect.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 5 min read

Twelve a month is an oddly persistent number. Three a week, one per working day if you are ambitious, "consistent publishing cadence" in the deck that got the budget approved.

Almost nobody arrives at it by calculation. It gets inherited — from an agency proposal, from a competitor's apparent output, from a benchmark report published by a company that sells software priced per published asset. Then it becomes the plan, and the plan becomes the thing the team is measured against, and within two quarters the cadence is the strategy.

This piece is arithmetic rather than opinion. Get a spreadsheet, use your own numbers, and see what you find.

Do the arithmetic on your own cadence

Take your annual content spend. Include everything: writer or agency fees, the fraction of a marketer's salary spent commissioning and editing, design time, the SME hours your engineers and product people give up in reviews. That last one is routinely omitted and is often the largest line.

Divide by the number of pieces you published. That is your true cost per piece, and it is usually two to three times the invoice figure once internal time is counted.

Now the number that matters. Go through last year's published pieces and mark every one that did at least one of these things:

  • Ranked and brought traffic that converted to something
  • Was sent by a salesperson to a live opportunity
  • Was cited or shared by someone outside the company whose opinion you value
  • Changed how a prospect understood your product in a way you can point to

Divide total spend by that number.

The gap between the two figures is the actual finding. In most audits I have run, a small minority of pieces account for nearly all the useful outcomes. So a company that believes it is paying a certain amount per article is in fact paying many multiples of that per article that did anything, and the rest is a subsidy paid to keep the cadence intact.

An illustration with round numbers, which are mine rather than yours: at 144 pieces a year and $400 all-in per piece, that is $57,600. If twelve of them did any of the four things above, the real figure is $4,800 per useful piece. The same budget spent on twenty-four commissioned pieces at $2,400 each would need only two of them to work to break even on that comparison — and pieces that cost $2,400 tend to involve customer interviews and specialist time, which is precisely what makes them work.

The arithmetic is not subtle. It is just rarely performed.

The costs that never make it into the calculation

Three of them, all real, none of them on any invoice.

Attention debt from your own experts. Every piece needs SME input. At high cadence, your engineers and product people are asked for review time constantly, and they learn to give it perfunctorily because they cannot give it properly twelve times a month. The result is that the pieces which genuinely needed their attention do not get it either. You spend the same expert hours and get less from all of them.

The quality floor becomes the quality ceiling. Cadence targets create a standing question — is this good enough to ship? — and the answer, under deadline, is always yes. Repeated a hundred times, "good enough to ship" becomes the house standard. Nobody decides this. It accretes.

Site-level dilution. A large library of thin pages on the same topic is not neutral. Internal linking spreads across mediocre pages instead of concentrating on strong ones. Similar pieces compete for the same queries. And a visitor who arrives, reads something forgettable and leaves has been given evidence about your company, which is worse than having been given none.

What compounding actually requires

The strongest argument for high cadence is compounding: content is one of the few marketing investments that appreciates, so more of it earlier means more compound growth later.

That argument is correct, and it is regularly misapplied, because compounding requires conditions that volume alone does not create.

Pieces compound when they are connected — when they build a topical position, link into each other deliberately, and point at a commercial destination. Ninety disconnected posts do not compound. They are ninety separate small expenses that happen to share a domain.

Pieces also compound when they are good enough to be referenced. The mechanism of compounding is other people citing you, sales teams reusing you, and a page holding a position long enough to accumulate authority. Forgettable pieces are not referenced by anyone, including your own team.

So the real choice is not more versus fewer. It is connected and referenceable versus neither. Twelve connected, referenceable pieces a month would be excellent. Almost no small team can produce that, which is the actual constraint everyone is working around.

When high cadence is genuinely right

It sometimes is, and the cases are identifiable.

Programmatic or templated content over a real dataset. If you have a legitimate structured data source — integrations, locations, comparisons, supported configurations — then hundreds of pages can each be genuinely useful to a narrow query. This is a different discipline from blogging and should not be budgeted from the same line.

Documentation and product-adjacent content. Volume here is the point. Every gap is a support ticket.

A news-adjacent category where recency is the value. Security advisories, regulatory change, model releases. If being early is what makes the piece useful, cadence is the strategy.

Genuinely resourced operations. A team with several full-time writers, a dedicated editor and real SME access can sustain high cadence without hitting the quality floor. If that describes you, this article is not about you.

What these have in common is that the volume serves a mechanism. The failure case is volume as a proxy for effort.

What this means for a startup or SME

If you have one marketer and a freelance budget, here is what I would do.

Cut the cadence in half and hold the budget flat. Not a reduction in investment — a reallocation from quantity to depth. The per-piece budget doubles, which is roughly what it takes to include a customer interview or real expert time.

Require a named job per piece, in one sentence, before commissioning. "Rank for X so that the reader learns Y and is then ready for Z." If nobody can write that sentence, the piece comes off the roadmap. This filter alone typically removes a third of a planned quarter.

Reserve one slot a quarter for something disproportionate. An original piece of research, a genuinely opinionated argument from your founder, a properly built case study. These are the pieces that get referenced, and being referenced is the only mechanism by which any of this compounds.

Re-run the arithmetic in six months. Same calculation, same four criteria. If the cost per useful piece has not fallen, something else is wrong and you now have the evidence to go find it.

Nobody has ever lost a deal because a company published six articles that month instead of twelve. Plenty of companies have spent a year's budget discovering that consistency, by itself, is not a strategy.

Where this leaves you

If you can't say what each of last quarter's pieces was for, the cadence is the first thing to change.
