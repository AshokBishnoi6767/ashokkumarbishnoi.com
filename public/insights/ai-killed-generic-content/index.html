AI didn't kill content marketing. It killed generic content.

When the marginal cost of competent, forgettable prose falls to nearly zero, the middle of the market stops existing. That is an ordinary economic event with unusual consequences.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 5 min read

The predictions were wrong in a specific and instructive way. Content marketing did not die. Search did not collapse. Writers did not become unnecessary.

What happened was more ordinary than any of that, and it has happened in other markets many times: the marginal cost of one particular good fell to approximately zero, and everything priced on the assumption that it wouldn't had to re-price.

The good in question was competent, forgettable prose. That was always the bulk of the market.

What actually got commoditised

Before this, producing an article that was grammatically clean, structurally sensible, on-topic and unremarkable required a human being and several hours. It was not skilled work at the top of the profession, but it was real work, and it had a real price.

An enormous share of B2B content spend went into exactly that. The definitional explainer. The listicle. The competent summary of a topic already covered by six other companies. The 1,500-word piece whose function was to exist at a URL so that a keyword had somewhere to land.

That entire category now costs close to nothing to produce, at unlimited volume, at a quality level indistinguishable from the median human output that preceded it. Anyone can verify this in an afternoon.

When the marginal cost of a good goes to zero, three things follow with some reliability. Supply expands until the good is worthless. Anything whose value derived from being scarce loses that value. And anything the commodity cannot substitute for becomes disproportionately more valuable, because the alternatives have vanished.

All three are visible now.

The middle of the market stopped existing

Content used to sit on a rough spectrum. At one end, genuinely original work: primary research, real expertise, first-hand experience, a point of view someone would argue with. At the other, filler. In between — where most of the money was — a large middle band of solid, professional, unremarkable content that was better than filler and cheaper than originality.

That middle band is gone. Not declining: gone. Anything a model can produce from what it already knows has no economic reason to be commissioned from a person, and there is no version of that trade that comes back.

What this means practically is that the safe strategic position no longer exists. For fifteen years, "produce reliably decent content consistently" was a defensible plan. It required no unusual talent and no access to anything scarce. It worked because everyone else's decent content was also expensive, so consistency alone created separation.

Now consistency is free for everyone, which means it separates nobody. The only remaining positions are genuinely original, or invisible.

What got more valuable

The inverse of the commoditised thing. Specifically, work that required access to something a model did not have.

  • Primary access. Your customer's actual experience, obtained by interviewing them. Your engineers' reasoning about why they built it this way. Your founder's account of the decision they got wrong in 2022. None of this exists in a training corpus. All of it is obtainable by anyone willing to schedule the call.
  • Position. A model can summarise the range of views on a question. It is structurally poor at holding one and defending it, because it is optimised toward the consensus of what it has read. An argument someone would dispute is now a differentiator by construction.
  • Judgement about what not to publish. When production is free, the constraint moves entirely to selection. Deciding that eleven of the twelve proposed pieces should not exist is now a higher-value activity than writing any of them.
  • Verification. Fluent and wrong is a much cheaper output than it used to be, and technical audiences punish it severely. Someone who can tell whether an explanation of your product is actually correct is worth more than someone who can write it beautifully.

Notice that only one of these four is a writing skill. That is the actual shift.

Where the tools genuinely help

I use them, and pretending otherwise would be silly. It is worth being precise about where they earn their place, because the honest answer is narrower than the marketing and wider than the backlash.

They are good at the parts of the work that are structurally repetitive and verifiable: reformatting a long piece for a different channel, generating twenty headline options so you can reject nineteen, summarising a transcript you are about to work from, first-pass research you will then check, drafting the boring connective sections of a document whose argument you have already built.

They are bad — not weak, bad — at the parts where the value is: deciding what is worth saying, holding a position under pressure, knowing which of two true statements matters more to this specific buyer, and noticing that the explanation you were given by the product team does not hold together.

The failure mode I see most often is not people using these tools. It is people using them at the wrong end of the process: automating the thinking and hand-finishing the prose, when the productive arrangement is the reverse.

The honest counterargument

There is a version of this argument I do not fully believe, and it is worth naming.

The optimistic case says that as generic content floods every channel, discovery mechanisms will adapt to reward originality, and quality will win on its merits. Perhaps. But discovery mechanisms have historically adapted slowly and imperfectly, and there is a real interim period — possibly a long one — where the flood simply makes everything harder to find, including the good work.

If that is where we are, then the practical implication is uncomfortable: original content is now necessary but not sufficient. It also needs distribution that does not depend entirely on an algorithm choosing to surface it. Which means owned audience, direct relationships, and the founder's own credibility carrying pieces into circulation.

That is a harder programme than publishing twelve articles a month. It is also the one that is not about to be replicated at zero cost by a competitor.

What this means for a startup or SME

Three concrete reallocations.

Move budget from volume to access. The money that used to buy twelve generic articles buys four pieces built on customer interviews and engineer time. The interviews are the expensive part and the reason the output cannot be replicated.

Stop commissioning anything a model could write from public knowledge. A clean test at the brief stage: could this piece be produced without speaking to anyone at your company or any of your customers? If yes, do not commission it. Someone has already published it, or will have by next week.

Get your founder's actual opinions on record. This is the single most defensible asset a small technology company has, and it is currently sitting unexploited in most of them. It is not replicable, it is not commoditised, and it costs an hour of their time per piece.

The companies that will struggle are the ones whose content advantage was volume and consistency. The companies that will do well are the ones that were already too small to compete on volume and are therefore forced to compete on something that still works.

Where this leaves you

The work that survives this is the work that required access to something a model didn't have.
