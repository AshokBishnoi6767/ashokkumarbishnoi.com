Why most B2B thought leadership isn't thought leadership.

A working definition, a one-question test, and the three failure modes that produce everything filed under the label but not meeting it.

By Ashok Kumar Bishnoi · 5 September 2026 · 5 min read

Contents

  2. The one-question test
  3. Three failure modes
  4. The obvious objection
  5. What this means for a startup or SME

All insights

Most of what B2B technology companies file under "thought leadership" contains no thought and leads nothing. This is not a controversial observation among people who read a lot of it. What is less often examined is why, given that everyone involved is capable and well-intentioned.

Part of the answer is that the term has no working definition. It is used to mean "long", "not obviously promotional", or "written by someone senior". None of those is a definition, and without one there is no way to tell whether a piece succeeded.

So let me propose one, and a test that goes with it.

A working definition

Thought leadership is a public argument, made from first-hand experience, that a reasonable and informed person in your industry could disagree with.

Three conditions, all load-bearing.

An argument. Not a survey of the landscape, not a summary of what is happening, not a list of considerations. A claim that something is true, or should be done, with reasons attached. If you cannot state it as a sentence with a verb that asserts something, it is not an argument.

From first-hand experience. The evidence has to be something you have access to and the reader does not: what you have built, what you have watched fail, what your customers told you, what you got wrong. Analysis assembled entirely from other people's published work is commentary. Commentary is a legitimate genre; it is just not this one.

Disputable by a reasonable expert. This is the condition that eliminates almost everything. If a knowledgeable practitioner could read your piece and say "well obviously," you have written a description of consensus. Consensus already has a leader.

The one-question test

Take any piece and ask: who, specifically, would argue with this?

Not "would someone hypothetically disagree" — name a role, a company type, a school of thought. If you can name them and articulate what they would say back, you have an argument. If the honest answer is "nobody, really," you have a description.

This test is uncomfortable to apply to your own drafts, which is exactly why it works. It also has a useful second-order property: pieces that pass it are far more likely to be shared, quoted and remembered, because disagreement is one of the few reliable mechanisms by which ideas travel.

Applied honestly to most published B2B thought leadership, the pass rate is low. Here is why.

Three failure modes

The summary

The most common. A piece that accurately describes what is happening in a market, cites some recent developments, and observes that companies will need to adapt.

Everything in it is true. Nothing in it is contestable. The author has done real work — reading, synthesising, organising — and produced something with no position in it.

This happens because summarising is genuinely difficult and feels like thinking. It also feels safer, since a summary cannot be wrong. But a piece that cannot be wrong also cannot be right in any way that matters, and the reader finishes it having learned what is happening without learning what anyone should do about it.

The hedge

Here the writer has an argument and then systematically removes the parts that could be challenged.

You can watch it happen in the language. "X is dead" becomes "X is evolving". "You should stop doing Y" becomes "Y may not be right for every organisation". A specific number becomes "significant". A named example becomes "one company we worked with".

Each individual softening is defensible. The cumulative effect is a piece that asserts nothing. The hedge usually comes from a reasonable fear — of being wrong publicly, of alienating a prospect, of a competitor screenshotting the strong version. Those risks are real. They are also much smaller than the risk of publishing something nobody remembers, which is a certainty rather than a risk.

The committee

The most expensive, because it destroys work that was good when it started.

A founder gives a strong interview. A draft comes back with a genuine claim in it. Then it goes to marketing, who soften the framing; to legal, who remove the specific example; to a product lead, who adds a paragraph about a feature; and to a second executive, who requests a section for balance.

Everyone acted reasonably within their remit. The output has no author. It reads like an organisation talking, and organisations do not have points of view — people do. Readers detect this immediately, even when they could not articulate what they detected.

The committee failure is structural rather than editorial, which means it cannot be fixed by better writing. It is fixed by deciding, in advance, who has final say and giving that to one named person.

The obvious objection

"We are a small company selling to enterprises. We cannot afford to publish contentious opinions."

This deserves a serious answer rather than a dismissal, because there is something in it.

The something is that contentious and disputable are not the same thing. You do not need to attack competitors, take political positions, or be provocative for its own sake. Provocation as a tactic is its own failure mode and reads as insecure.

What you need is a genuine technical or commercial position. "Most companies in this category should not buy software like ours yet, and here is how to tell whether you are one of them" is disputable, useful, and makes you more credible to enterprise buyers rather than less. "We think the industry's standard approach to this problem is wrong, and here is the architecture argument for why" is disputable and will be read carefully by exactly the technical evaluators you want.

Enterprise buyers are not put off by companies with positions. They are put off by companies that appear to have none, because a vendor with no position is difficult to evaluate and easy to replace.

What this means for a startup or SME

You have a structural advantage here that you are probably not using.

Your founder has spent years accumulating opinions the market has not caught up with. They are not in the content because nobody has extracted them, and because your founder does not think of them as opinions — they think of them as obvious facts about how the work goes.

Three things to do about that.

Find the disagreements first, not the topics. Ask your founder what most people in the industry believe that they think is wrong; what they do differently from every competitor and why; what they believed three years ago and have since abandoned. Those three questions typically produce a year of material in under an hour.

Give one person the final edit. Ideally the person whose name is on it. Review for accuracy and legal exposure, absolutely. Review for tone by consensus, never.

Apply the test before publishing, not after. Who specifically would argue with this? If nobody, do not publish it as thought leadership. It might still be a perfectly good explainer, and calling it what it is costs nothing.

A piece that some of your market disagrees with is doing the job. A piece that all of your market nods at was not worth the time it took to read.

Where this leaves you

If your founder has an opinion the market would argue with, that is the whole programme. It just needs extracting.
